Image Compressor

Shrink JPG, PNG and WebP files with a quality slider, and see exactly how many bytes each one lost. Files never leave your browser.

How it compresses

Each image is decoded, drawn onto a canvas at the size you asked for, and re-encoded by the browser's own image encoder. That re-encode is where the saving happens: JPEG and WebP are lossy formats, so lowering the quality setting discards detail the eye is unlikely to notice while removing a large amount of data.

PNG is different. It is a lossless format, so the quality slider does not apply to it — PNG output is only smaller when you also reduce the pixel dimensions. If you want real savings on a photograph, choose JPEG or WebP.

Choosing a quality setting

  • 90–100 — visually indistinguishable from the original for most photos, but the file may barely shrink. The last few percent of quality costs a disproportionate number of bytes.
  • 70–85 — the practical sweet spot for photographs published on the web. Typically 60–85% smaller than the original camera file with no visible loss at normal viewing sizes.
  • 50–65 — noticeable on close inspection, acceptable for thumbnails and background images.
  • Below 50 — visible blocky artefacts around edges and in gradients. Only for small previews.

JPEG, WebP or PNG?

FormatBest forTransparency
JPEGPhotographs, anywhere compatibility mattersNo
WebPEverything on the modern web; 25–35% smaller than JPEG at equal qualityYes

Note the transparency warning: JPEG cannot store an alpha channel, so transparent areas are filled with white here. If your image has a transparent background, use WebP or PNG instead.

Why compressing twice is a bad idea

Every lossy re-encode discards a little more detail, permanently. Compressing an already-compressed JPEG at 70% does not halve the file again — it adds another generation of artefacts on top of the ones already there. Always compress from the original file if you can, and keep a copy of the original.

FAQ

Are my images uploaded?

No. Decoding and encoding both happen in this tab with the Canvas API. You can disconnect from the internet after the page loads and it will keep working.

Why did my PNG get bigger?

The browser's PNG encoder may use different compression settings than the tool that made your original. For a large flat-colour PNG, exporting as WebP instead usually wins.

Is there a file size limit?

Only your device's memory. Very large images — above roughly 50 megapixels — may fail on a canvas size limit that browsers impose for safety.

Can I compress many images at once?

Yes. Select multiple files, adjust the settings once, and press Compress. Use Download all to save them together — your browser may ask permission to download multiple files.

Word & Character Counter

Live counts of words, characters, sentences and paragraphs, plus estimated reading time and the most frequent words.

What is counted

  • Words — runs of non-whitespace separated by spaces. Hyphenated compounds such as well-known count as one word.
  • Characters — every character in the text, and separately every character excluding spaces, tabs and line breaks. Which number matters depends on the limit you are writing against.
  • Sentences — counted by terminal punctuation: full stops, question marks, exclamation marks and ellipses.
  • Paragraphs — blocks separated by a blank line.
  • Reading time — based on 225 words per minute, a common average for adult silent reading.

Which character count do platforms actually use?

This trips people up constantly. Meta descriptions and tweets count characters including spaces. Many academic and publishing guidelines count excluding spaces. When a platform shows a live counter as you type, it is almost always the number with spaces. Both are shown here so you never have to guess.

Common limits worth knowing

  • Title tags — around 60 characters before Google truncates.
  • Meta descriptions — around 155–160 characters.
  • Tweets — 280 characters including spaces.
  • Instagram captions — 2,200 characters.
  • LinkedIn posts — 3,000 characters.
  • YouTube titles — 100 characters.

FAQ

Is my text sent anywhere?

No. Every count is computed in JavaScript as you type. The text never leaves the page, and the tool works with the network disconnected.

Why does my word count differ from my editor's?

Editors disagree about edge cases: hyphenated words, numbers, emoji, and text inside tables or footnotes. Microsoft Word, for example, counts some punctuation-adjacent strings differently from a plain space-split.

Does it count Chinese or Japanese text correctly?

For scripts written without spaces, the space-based word count returns the number of text runs rather than meaningful words. The character count is exact, and for Chinese it is normally the number that matters — a common guideline is characters rather than words.

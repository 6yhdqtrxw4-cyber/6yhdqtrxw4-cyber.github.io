The five questions people actually ask

Almost every percentage problem is one of these, and mixing them up is the usual source of wrong answers:

  • "What is 15% of 240?" — multiply. 15 ÷ 100 × 240 = 36.
  • "45 is what percent of 180?" — divide, then multiply by 100. 45 ÷ 180 × 100 = 25%. The trap is dividing the wrong way round; the number after "of" is always the denominator.
  • "Prices went from 80 to 100 — by how much?" — the change divided by the starting value. (100 − 80) ÷ 80 × 100 = +25%. Using the final value as the denominator is the single most common percentage mistake.
  • "200 increased by −15%" — multiply by 1 + (−15/100), giving 170. A negative percentage here is just a decrease.
  • "30 is 12% of what?" — divide by the rate. 30 ÷ 0.12 = 250.

Why increases and decreases are not symmetric

A 50% loss needs a 100% gain to recover. Drop from 100 to 50 — that is −50% — and getting back to 100 requires +100%, because the percentages are measured against different bases. This asymmetry is why "the market fell 30%, then rose 30%" does not leave you where you started: 100 → 70 → 91, still down 9%.

The same reasoning explains why a discount and a markup do not cancel. Adding 20% then taking 20% off gives 96, not 100.

Percentage points are not percentages

If a rate rises from 4% to 5%, that is an increase of one percentage point but a 25% relative increase (1 ÷ 4). Both statements are true and they sound very different, which is exactly why the distinction gets abused in reporting. The rule: use percentage points when comparing two percentages; use percent when comparing a value to its own starting point.

FAQ

How do I add a percentage to a number quickly?

Multiply by one plus the rate. To add 8%, multiply by 1.08. To subtract 8%, multiply by 0.92. Doing the addition and subtraction in separate steps invites rounding errors.

What is the difference between percent and per mille?

Per mille (‰) is tenths of a percent: 5‰ is 0.5%. It is used for things like birth rates and blood alcohol limits, where percentages are inconveniently small.

Can a percentage change exceed 100%?

Increases can — going from 10 to 35 is +250%. Decreases cannot go below −100%, since that would mean passing zero into a negative value.

Why does the calculator show a formula?

So you can verify the result rather than trust it. Every number here comes from the browser's own arithmetic, but a formula you can read is the difference between using a tool and depending on it.
